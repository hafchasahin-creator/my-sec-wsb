Why the Moon Changes Shape

The Moon is always half lit. What changes is where you're standing.

Sunlight lights up exactly one half of the Moon, all day, every day, forever. As the Moon travels around Earth, we look at that same lit half from a new angle each night — and that changing angle is the whole reason the Moon looks like a sliver, a half, or a full circle.

Why do we see phases?

Start with the part that never changes. The Sun is so far away that its light reaches the Moon as a bundle of straight, parallel rays, and those rays can only ever hit one side of a ball. So exactly half of the Moon is lit up, right now and always — a permanent day side and a permanent night side. The line running around the Moon between the two is called the terminator, which just means the border between the sunlit part and the dark part.

Now add the movement. The Moon takes about 29.5 days to travel all the way around Earth, and it carries that lit half with it — the lit half always points at the Sun, like a sunflower that never looks away. Because we are riding along on Earth in the middle, we end up looking at that same lit half from a different angle every single night.

That angle is the phase. When the Moon is on the far side of Earth from the Sun, the lit half points straight at us and we see a full circle. When the Moon is between us and the Sun, the lit half points away and we see almost nothing. In between, we catch the lit half side-on and see a slice of it — a crescent, a half, or a fat gibbous shape. Nothing covers the Moon and nothing is switched off. We are simply looking at the same lit half from a new seat.

How to think about it

  1. Half of the Moon is lit. That half never gets bigger or smaller.
  2. The lit half always faces the Sun, wherever the Moon has travelled to.
  3. In one month the Moon circles Earth, so we view that lit half from every angle.
  4. The phase you see is just how much of the lit half is turned toward you.

Same Moon. Same sunlight. Different angle. That's the whole trick.

Common mix-up

Phases are NOT Earth's shadow

Lots of people are told that the Moon's phases happen because Earth's shadow falls across the Moon. It is a tidy idea, and it is wrong. Earth's shadow points straight away from the Sun, so it can only ever reach the Moon at one moment in the cycle — Full Moon — and even then it usually misses. Phases, meanwhile, keep changing smoothly all month long. A shadow cannot explain that.

Earth's shadow does hit the Moon sometimes, and when it does we call it a lunar eclipse. Earth blocks the sunlight, the Moon slides into the dark cone behind us, and for a couple of hours it dims and turns a deep coppery red. That red light is sunrise and sunset from all around Earth's edge, bent through our atmosphere and spilling onto the Moon.

So why isn't there an eclipse every single Full Moon? Because the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5° compared with Earth's path around the Sun. Five degrees sounds tiny, but across that distance it is enough: at most Full Moons the Moon sails above or below Earth's shadow and sails right past it. The two only line up now and then. Somewhere on Earth there are usually two lunar eclipses a year, sometimes three — and from any one town, a total lunar eclipse is a treat you get roughly every two or three years.

How to tell them apart

When
A phase is on show every night of the month. An eclipse only happens at Full Moon, a couple of times a year.
How long
A phase changes too slowly to watch; you compare one night with the next. An eclipse runs its whole course in about two hours, and you can watch the edge move.
Edge and colour
In a phase the dark part is just unlit and disappears into the sky, behind a clean, smooth curve. In an eclipse the edge is a soft, fuzzy arc from a much larger circle, and the covered part glows dim orange-red.

Waxing and waning

Waxing means the lit part we can see is growing bigger each night, from New Moon up to Full Moon.

Waning means the lit part we can see is shrinking each night, from Full Moon back down to New Moon.

"Wax" is an old word for grow — the same idea as wax building up on a candle — and "wane" is an old word for shrink.

The D-O-C trick (Northern Hemisphere)

Watch the shape of the Moon and read it like a letter. If the Moon looks like a D, it is waxing and heading for Full. A round O is the Full Moon itself. If it looks like a C, it is waning and heading for New. D, then O, then C — in that order, right through the month.

It works because from the Northern Hemisphere the bright edge sits on the right while the Moon is growing, and on the left while it is shrinking.

Heads up: south of the equator you are looking at the sky "upside down", so the whole thing flips. In the Southern Hemisphere a waxing Moon looks like a C and a waning Moon looks like a D.

One month, start to finish

One full cycle, from New Moon to the next New Moon, takes about 29.5 days. That is where our word "month" comes from — it is really a "moonth".
