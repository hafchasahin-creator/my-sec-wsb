The Grandfather Paradox

Travel back in time and kill your own grandfather before he meets your grandmother. Then you were never born — so you never travelled back — so he lives. The story cannot be finished. This page animates exactly where it breaks, and what has to give.

The setup

A story that eats itself

You build a time machine, travel to 1940, and shoot your grandfather while he is still a teenager — years before he meets your grandmother.

Stated in this form by the French journalist René Barjavel in Le Voyageur Imprudent (1943), though the shape of the puzzle is older.

The paradox is not that this would be sad, or difficult, or that the timeline would "feel weird". It is that the story asserts a plain logical contradiction: a single event both happens and does not happen. Any theory of time travel has to say something about it — and every answer costs something.

The logic

Why it is a real contradiction, not just a weird feeling

Strip the story of guns and grandfathers and you are left with four claims. Each one looks innocent. Together they are inconsistent — so at least one has to be false.

P1

You exist in 2025 and step into the machine. Something has to do the travelling.

P2

You exist only because your grandfather survived to have children. That is the causal chain that produced you.

P3

The past is a place you can act in. Arriving in 1940 with a loaded gun, you have the same freedom you have anywhere else.

P4

An event either happened or it didn't. "Grandfather dies in 1940" cannot be both true and false of the same 1940.

P1 + P2 + P3 + P4 cannot all be true. The paradox is the bill for keeping all four.

Follow the ring: every state implies the next, and four steps later you are back at the state you just denied. The loop never settles — that is the paradox in one picture.

The escapes

Four ways out — and what each one costs

Each resolution rejects exactly one of the four claims above. Nobody gets to keep all of them.

Rejects P3 · Novikov

Self-consistency

Time travel is allowed, but only histories that are already consistent can occur. You can go to 1940 and you can aim — you just always miss. The gun jams, your hand shakes, you shoot the wrong man. Not because a force stops you, but because the inconsistent versions of the story were never among the possible histories in the first place. Whatever you do in 1940, you already did.

Cost: your freedom in the past becomes strangely constrained — an unbroken run of coincidences with no local explanation.
Rejects P4 · Branching

Many worlds / branching time

Arriving in 1940 splits history. You kill the grandfather of that branch, and on that branch you are indeed never born — but you still exist, as an immigrant from a timeline that continues untouched. Nothing is contradicted, because "1940" now names two different events on two different branches. The Deutsch model of quantum time travel makes a version of this precise.

Cost: you never really changed your past. You can't go home, and the grandfather you cared about is still alive somewhere.
Rejects the machine

Chronology protection

General relativity does permit closed timelike curves — Gödel's rotating universe, the interior of a spinning black hole, traversable wormholes. Hawking's chronology protection conjecture says quantum effects always intervene first: vacuum fluctuations pile up without bound at the moment a time machine would switch on and destroy it. The universe is "safe for historians."

Cost: still a conjecture. It needs a theory of quantum gravity to be settled either way.
Rejects P1 · Metaphysics

There is nothing to change

On a block-universe view, the whole of spacetime simply is. "Changing the past" asks for a second version of 1940 to replace the first — but there is no extra time dimension for that replacement to happen in. A time traveller is part of history, not an editor of it. The paradox dissolves because its central verb was never coherent.

Cost: it makes the puzzle go away by denying the intuition that motivated it — that the past could have gone otherwise.
Nearby puzzles

Its relatives

The bootstrap paradox

You travel back and hand Shakespeare the complete text of Hamlet, which he publishes, and which you later read and carry back. The loop is perfectly consistent — no contradiction anywhere. But who wrote it? The information has no origin. Self-consistency saves the story from contradiction; it does not explain where the play came from.

The predestination paradox

You rush back to prevent the fire — and it is your lantern, knocked over in the struggle, that starts it. Your attempt to change the past turns out to have caused it. This is what Novikov consistency looks like from the inside, and it is not a contradiction, only an unsettling one.

The "autoinfanticide" sharpening

Philosophers usually replace the grandfather with yourself as a baby, closing the loophole that you might have been adopted, or descended from someone else. Same logic, no escape hatch: it forces the question of whether "can" means "is physically able to" or "is consistent with everything else that is true".

Where this leaves us

The paradox is an argument, not a story

Its real force is as a piece of reasoning about what is possible. If backwards time travel allowed you to bring about a contradiction, then backwards time travel is impossible — so anyone who wants to keep it has to explain why the contradiction can never actually be built. Every serious answer does that in one of two ways: it restricts what you are able to do once you arrive, or it denies that you arrived in your own past at all. Nothing in known physics forbids closed timelike curves outright. What forbids the paradox is not physics but logic — and logic doesn't negotiate.
