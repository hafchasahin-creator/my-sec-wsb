What is an ecosystem?

An ecosystem is every living thing in a place — plants, animals, fungi, microbes — together with the non‑living conditions they depend on: sunlight, water, air, soil, and temperature. Neither half works alone. Remove the organisms and the physical setting is just geology; remove the physical setting and there's nothing left for organisms to live in. An ecosystem is the interaction itself.

Scale doesn't define it. A puddle, a fallen log, a coral reef, and the entire biosphere are all ecosystems — what matters is that energy and matter are actively cycling through a web of relationships.

Understory

Biotic components & the one‑way flow of energy

Producers (plants, algae, some bacteria) convert sunlight into chemical energy. Consumers eat that energy onward — herbivores eat producers, carnivores eat other consumers, omnivores do both. Decomposers — fungi and bacteria — break down the dead and the discarded, releasing nutrients back into the soil.

Energy only moves one direction, and it leaks at every step: roughly 90% of the energy an organism consumes is spent on staying alive and is lost as heat, not passed on. Only about 10% is available to whatever eats it next — which is why food chains rarely run past four or five links, and why there are always far more plants than lions.

Beyond a single chain

Real ecosystems are webs, not chains

Almost nothing eats only one thing. A fox eats rabbits, mice, and berries; a hawk eats mice, snakes, and grasshoppers. Overlap these chains and you get a food web — which is why losing a single species rarely stays isolated. Tap a node below to trace what depends on it, directly and in turn.

Matter doesn't disappear — it cycles

Energy flows through an ecosystem once and is gone. Matter is different: the same water, carbon, and nitrogen atoms move through living things and back into the environment endlessly. The clearest example is the water cycle.

Carbon cycle

Plants pull CO₂ from the air to build tissue; animals release it back by breathing; decomposers and fires release what's left in dead matter.

Nitrogen cycle

Soil bacteria "fix" nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use; decomposers return it to the soil when organisms die.

Ecosystems of the world

The same rules — energy flowing, matter cycling, biotic meeting abiotic — play out differently depending on climate, water, and geography. A handful of major biomes cover almost the entire planet.

Terrestrial

Forest

Layered canopy, understory, and floor pack more species into a hectare than almost any other biome.

Balance is interdependence, not stillness

A healthy ecosystem isn't unchanging — it's resilient, able to absorb disturbance and settle back into rhythm. That resilience comes entirely from how densely everything depends on everything else. Pull one thread and the whole web feels it.

Case in point

Wolves in Yellowstone. When wolves were removed in the 1920s, elk herds grew unchecked and overgrazed young willow and aspen along the rivers. Riverbanks eroded, beavers lost the wood they needed for dams, and the wetlands beavers create for fish, birds, and amphibians shrank with them. Reintroducing wolves in 1995 didn't just reduce elk numbers — it changed elk behavior, moving them out of vulnerable valleys, letting vegetation and riverbanks recover, and beavers return. One predator, restored, reshaped rivers.

Human activity now moves faster than most ecosystems can absorb: habitat converted to farmland or cities, waterways altered, and a climate warming faster than many species can migrate or adapt to. None of this ends life on Earth — ecosystems are old hands at recovering from disruption. But the specific, richly connected systems we depend on today are not guaranteed to be the ones we get back.
