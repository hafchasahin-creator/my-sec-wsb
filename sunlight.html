Sunlight's Journey

Every warm patch of sunlight on your skin began as light deep inside the Sun. This is the simple story of what sunlight actually is — and the surprisingly long trip it takes to reach you.

01 — The basics

What is sunlight, really?

Sunlight looks plain: a warm, whitish-yellow glow. But it isn't just one thing. It's made of tiny packets of energy called photons, and hiding inside that "white" glow is an entire rainbow — plus light your eyes can't even see.

Red Orange Yellow Green Blue Indigo Violet Ultraviolet Infrared

Split sunlight with a prism — or look at a rainbow — and the hidden colors appear. Ultraviolet (which can tan or burn skin) and infrared (which we feel as heat) ride along too. We just can't see them.

02 — The source

Born in a furnace, 15 million °C hot

Sunlight is made deep in the Sun's core, where the crush of gravity is so strong that hydrogen atoms fuse together into helium. This process, called nuclear fusion, releases enormous bursts of energy — light and heat.

But that light doesn't just zoom straight out into space. First, it has to escape the Sun itself, crossing several crowded layers on the way to the surface:

04 — Quick facts

The numbers, all in one place

299,792 km/s Speed of light

About 300,000 km every second — the fastest anything can travel in the universe.

150,000,000 km Sun-to-Earth distance

Also called 1 Astronomical Unit (AU) — roughly 93 million miles.

8 min 20 sec Time to cross space

How "old" the sunlight on your skin is right now, once it finally left the Sun.

~170,000 years Time trapped inside the Sun

Estimates vary a lot — from thousands of years to around this figure — for a photon to random-walk out of the core.

8 minutes If the Sun vanished

We wouldn't notice it had gone dark until the last sunlight already on its way finally arrived.

So the next time sunlight lands on your skin, remember: it spent longer trapped inside the Sun than humans have had writing — and still made the 150-million-kilometre dash to you in under nine minutes.
